Overview

Payments are not just a checkout feature.

At Storebox, a booking starts a financial lifecycle that can continue through recurring payments, invoices, refunds, payouts, reconciliation, failed-payment recovery, dunning and debt collection. My product work touches many parts of that lifecycle.

The challenge is that these workflows are tightly connected: changing payment behaviour can affect customer communication, invoicing, Finance operations, reconciliation, Customer Support and legal processes.

My role is often to understand how these pieces interact, define the expected product and system behaviour, coordinate with Engineering and external providers, and make sure financial and customer consequences are considered together.

Working with providers

I work with Engineering and providers on API migrations, request/response behaviour, provider limitations, transaction references, webhooks where relevant, error states, payment status changes, QA and production issue investigation. I don’t implement the payment APIs myself; I define how the product should behave around them.

How I approached it

Follow the full lifecycle

A payment decision should never be evaluated only at checkout. For each change: what does the customer see, what does the provider do, what does Finance receive, what invoice or credit-note behaviour changes, what happens in reconciliation, and what happens if the payment fails later?

Design failure states intentionally

Payments fail. Providers return unexpected responses. Customers change payment methods. Retries fail. Refunds and payouts can create reconciliation problems. These states need explicit product behaviour instead of manual workarounds.

Make financial data traceable

Provider transactions, payouts, refunds and internal bookings need to be understandable enough for Finance to reconcile them. That requires clear references and relationships between customer, booking, payment, invoice, refund, payout and provider transaction.

Treat market-specific rules as product logic

Dunning and payment processes differ by country. Those differences need to become explicit rules in the product, not knowledge held informally by individual teams.

Key decisions

  • Design failure states, not only happy pathsFailed payments, retries, payment-method changes and provider errors need explicit behaviour.
  • Reconciliation must be possible by designPayments, refunds and payouts need references that let Finance trace them back to bookings and invoices.
  • Country-specific rules belong in the productDunning and recovery logic can’t rely on tribal knowledge when requirements differ by market.
  • Customer communication is part of the payment flowPayment failures, method changes, refunds and price changes need clear communication, not only backend processing.
  • Provider constraints become product constraintsWhen Stripe, PayPal, Klarna or banking flows behave differently, those limitations have to be translated into understandable customer and operational behaviour.
  • Auditability mattersFinancial and debt-collection processes need to make it clear what happened, when and why.

Technical and operational complexity

The complexity comes from coordinating several connected states:

The same customer can therefore have several connected states across different systems, and those states need to remain consistent.

Invoice generation, references and credit-note behaviour are part of the wider financial workflow, so payment changes often need to be evaluated together with invoicing.

Failed payments

A failed recurring payment starts another product journey rather than simply becoming an error. The questions it raises:

  • Should we retry, when, and how many times?
  • What does the customer see?
  • Can the customer change their payment method?
  • Does customer reapproval become necessary?
  • What communication is required?
  • At what point does the case move into dunning, and when into debt collection?

Reconciliation

Reconciliation is one of the areas where technical and financial product thinking meet. Provider transactions, payouts, refunds and internal records need to map back to the correct booking and invoice, so discrepancies can be investigated and Finance can trust the resulting records. That depends on provider references, internal booking references, invoice relationships, and clear handling of refunds and payouts.

Dunning and debt collection

When payment recovery fails, the workflow moves into country-specific dunning and debt-collection processes. I have worked on product rules around when cases are eligible for handover, which blockers prevent handover, what communication is required and how the process remains traceable, including the Netherlands WIK process with its 14-day legal flow and handover to Pair Finance.

Outcome

My work helps connect customer-facing payment experiences with the financial and operational processes that happen behind them.

By treating payments, invoicing, reconciliation, failed-payment recovery and debt collection as one connected lifecycle, product changes can be designed with fewer downstream surprises and clearer behaviour for customers and internal teams.

This has allowed me to work on payment problems not only as isolated technical issues, but as end-to-end product and business-process challenges.

Product principleA payment is not finished when the provider returns “success”. The product has to remain correct through invoicing, reconciliation, refunds and recovery.
Product principleIn payments, customer experience, financial correctness and system behaviour are the same problem viewed from different sides.

What I learned

Payments taught me to think beyond the visible customer journey. A change that looks small in checkout can create consequences in invoicing, reconciliation, Customer Support or debt collection.

The strongest payment products are designed across the full lifecycle rather than optimising one step in isolation.
